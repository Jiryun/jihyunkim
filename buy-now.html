Buy now

Could payment and shipping become a new growth lever for Karrot in Canada? We built the first version to find out, from kickoff to launch in six weeks.

GoalFind new growth for Karrot in Canada
TeamThe CEO, a backend engineer and me
My roleApproach, policy and specification, UX and UI
Timeline6 weeks, kickoff to launch. Live across Canada since Sep 16, 2026

The question

Karrot in Canada was a meetup-only marketplace. Only 7% of monthly users traded, about 70% of trades were under $50, and every trade was limited to how far people would travel to meet. Facebook Marketplace led on both buyers and sellers, and scams asking for an e-Transfer up front were common.

Shipping had already worked for Karrot elsewhere. In Korea, Karrot added Buy now with payment and parcel delivery in September 2025. By February 2026, completed Buy now trades had grown about 90 times, and in the first half of 2026, Karrot’s brokerage and payment revenue, which includes Buy now, was 19.4 times higher than a year earlier. In the US, Vinted showed the demand for shipped second-hand goods: its monthly app downloads rose from 288,000 in January 2026 to 2.9 million in August.

Canada had no one doing this yet. Facebook Marketplace offers checkout with shipping only in the US, and no major peer-to-peer app in Canada had shipping and payment built in. Before Buy now existed, 26,440 Karrot chats in Canada were already working out delivery on their own.

Delivery, not speed

The working title, Instant Buy, described a faster checkout. I reframed it. In an app that had only ever worked face to face, the real change was that an item could travel at all. That made Buy now a bet on supply, not a checkout feature.

The bet

I wrote the MVP PRD around one test: grow listings that can ship, and see whether trades grow with them.

Hypothesis Secure payment and shipping lower the barrier for higher-value trades. Shipping across Canada gives every neighbourhood more to buy.
What we measure Listings with shipping set up, and total listings for sale.
Guardrails Listing completion rate, and meetup trades, watched with a holdout so shipping adds to meetups instead of replacing them.
Not goals for v1 Payment conversion, and replacing meetups.
First target 100 transactions with no marketing or subsidies, then 1,000 a month with marketing.

How we decided

Every v1 choice had to fit four real-world limits: our partners, the business bet, cost and the law. Inside them, we looked for moments that make shipping feel easy. The pattern: take the option that ships fastest and keeps the money safe, and move risky or costly parts to later.

PartnersCanada Post and our payment partner each set limits we couldn’t change.

One carrier. Canada Post only, drop-off only, and one label per order.

The partner holds the money. Our payment partner holds each payment and checks identity only when it needs to. Its checkout screens can’t be customized, so we designed around them.

BusinessThe bet was supply, so every choice had to make sellers more likely to turn shipping on.

Shipping adds to meetups. Sellers choose Sell nationwide, which keeps meetups open. Listings say Shipping available, not Shipping only, and we never speak negatively about meeting up.

No extra seller fees. Buyers pay shipping and a Buyer Protection Fee. Sellers pay nothing extra.

All of Canada. We launched everywhere at once, rather than Toronto or Ontario first. The US stayed out of v1.

CostShipping, fees and payouts all had a price.

A $10 floor. Below $10, shipping and fees cost more than the item.

Drop-off only. Carrier pickup cost $3 or more per order and was hard to bill to the buyer.

Size before weight. Weight options start at 5 kg, because picking a size is easier than weighing. The cost is at most $1.59 undercharged on an order.

Legal and riskMoney between strangers brings fraud, chargebacks and consumer law.

Fees up front. Canada’s Competition Act bars mandatory fees that first appear at checkout. The Buyer Protection Fee is named on the listing, and we chose to explain it as clearly as we could, beyond the legal minimum.

A $1,000 cap. Above it, fraud and chargeback losses grow. It also matches Vinted’s Buyer Protection limit.

Interac payouts. Bank transfers can be reversed for up to 90 days, so sellers are paid by Interac e-Transfer, with Karrot covering the fee.

What can ship. We blocked only what Canada Post prohibits, plus helmets, instead of whole categories, so electronics, fragile items and baby gear could ship. I set the details: helmets as the only blocked protective gear, and pet food allowed for every species.

Moments that feel easyInside those limits, we looked for the moments that make shipping feel effortless.

One tap to buy. Buy now goes straight to an itemized checkout, with no back and forth in chat.

Protection without asking. Buyers have 48 hours after delivery to report a problem, and if the seller doesn’t reply in time, the refund is automatic.

Paid without chasing. Sellers are paid 48 hours after delivery, or as soon as the buyer confirms.

What we left for later

Each of these would have made Buy now better for someone. Each waited for a reason: a cost, a risk, or because it didn’t help answer the question.

Package size, with less to think about

Choosing a package size was the step most likely to stop a seller. The buyer’s shipping cost depends on it. And unlike the US Postal Service, Canada Post gives out no free standard boxes, so Canadians reuse delivery boxes or pick one up at a grocery or liquor store.

I studied how retail apps explain fit and how marketplaces describe box sizes, then went through rounds of drafts and tests. Each round asked less of the seller.

Round 1. Two sizes, each defined by an object and a weight limit.

Round 2. Three sizes with plain names, example items and exact limits, plus a size guide.

Shipped. Four sizes, each one thing people own. The likely size comes first, already selected.

A list of example items became one familiar object per size. Weight came off the size cards, since sellers pick a size or a weight, never both. And the size most items in the category need comes first, already selected.

No ruler needed. Printer paper for length and width, a thumb for height, and a shake test for padding. Sellers can also type in a box’s size to check it.

Heavy items, version A. Real items in each weight range.

Version B. One familiar anchor per range, like a 4 L jug of milk or a checked airline bag.

For heavy categories, sellers pick a weight instead, up to 30 kg. I tested two ways to make kilograms feel real: real items in each range, or one familiar anchor.

Behind each choice is what the label declares. Sizes use a dimensional weight, from 0.7 kg for S to 9.4 kg for XL, and weight ranges use their upper limit.

After launch: what users told us

In the first weeks, support tickets and user feedback kept coming back to the same questions: how shipping works from start to finish, who pays for it, when sellers get paid, and what to do after a sale. The follow-ups below answer those questions and make shipping easier to switch on.

How shipping works. One page from sale to payout, showing whose turn it is at each step.

Answers in place. Common questions, like who pays for shipping, open an AI help chat.

A timeline, not a chat. I chose one vertical timeline over chat-style bubbles. Sellers see whose turn it is, the next action, and when they’ll be paid.

A QR code before a printer. A label needs a printer, so sellers now show a QR code at the counter. Printing is the fallback.

Shipping for every listing. Sellers can turn on shipping for all their active listings at once, with the zero-fee promise up front.

Ask for shipping. Buyers can ask a meetup-only seller to turn on shipping, so the item can ship with their other items.

Bundles without surprise prices

For buying several items at once, I dropped the option where buyers approve a maximum and pay the real price later. An unknown price is a big barrier. If some items can’t ship, I chose to ask the buyer to keep the rest or cancel, rather than quietly removing items.

The problem under the problem

Better screens alone won’t make people buy and sell by shipping. The data showed two bigger problems: shipping listings sold less often than local ones, and too many orders were cancelled. I started a cancellation study that pairs order data with the order chats. 75 of 238 orders had been cancelled, with the most value lost on orders of $150 and up.

Where it stands

It’s too early to call Buy now a growth lever. Orders are growing and the reach is real, but supply is short and too many orders fall through. Numbers as of late October 2026.

Working
246 paid orders and CA$26.7k in sales since launch. The first target was 100.

Not yet

What’s next

Cancellations first: why orders fall through, from both the numbers and the chats. Then whether shipping listings can sell as well as local ones. The question hasn’t changed: can Buy now grow Karrot in Canada? Now we have a product to answer it with.
