Buy now

Buy now: Selling Nationwide

How do you introduce payments and shipping to a marketplace built entirely around face-to-face trades?

As the sole designer, I reframed the product opportunity, defined the MVP strategy and policies, and partnered directly with engineering to launch an end-to-end commerce experience across Canada in six weeks—using AI to extend our small team’s capabilities.

GoalFind new growth for Karrot in Canada
TeamThe CEO, a backend engineer and me
My roleSole designer: approach, policy, specification, UX and UI
Timeline6 weeks, kickoff to launch. Live across Canada since Sep 16, 2026

Context

Karrot in Canada was a meetup-only marketplace. Only 7% of monthly users traded, about 70% of trades were under $50, and every trade was limited to how far people would travel to meet.

Shipping had already worked for Karrot in Korea, where completed Buy now trades grew about 90 times between September 2025 and February 2026. In Canada, no major peer-to-peer app had shipping and payment built in, yet 26,440 Karrot chats were already working out delivery on their own.

Problem

Delivery, not speed

The working title, Instant Buy, described a faster checkout. I reframed it. In an app that had only ever worked face to face, the real change was that an item could travel at all. That made Buy now a bet on supply, not a checkout feature.

Goal

I wrote the MVP PRD around one test: grow listings that can ship, and see whether trades grow with them.

Hypothesis Secure payment and shipping lower the barrier for higher-value trades. Shipping across Canada gives every neighbourhood more to buy.
What we measure Listings with shipping set up, and total listings for sale.
Guardrails Listing completion rate, and meetup trades, watched with a holdout so shipping adds to meetups instead of replacing them.
Not goals for v1 Payment conversion, and replacing meetups.
First target 100 transactions with no marketing or subsidies, then 1,000 a month with marketing.

How I worked

The team was the CEO, a backend engineer and me, with no PM or data analyst. I used Claude to cover much of that ground, before launch and after it. I set the questions and made the calls.

Week 1 · Aug 3 Kickoff I mapped the user flow while engineering started on the seller screens.
Weeks 2 to 3 Spec complete I finalized the flows, spec, nudges and onboarding. Payment and shipping partners were integrated, and alpha QA began.
Weeks 4 to 5 Real parcels QA in production with real orders and parcels. A spec check caught 17 conflicts first. Support agents were trained, and the shipping rules set.
Sep 16 Launch Buy now went live across Canada, six weeks after kickoff.

Key decisions

Every v1 choice had to fit four real-world limits: our partners, the business bet, cost and the law. The pattern: take the option that ships fastest and keeps the money safe, and move risky or costly parts to later.

PartnersCanada Post and our payment partner each set limits we couldn’t change.

One carrier. Canada Post only, drop-off only, and one label per order.

The partner holds the money. Our payment partner holds each payment and checks identity only when it needs to. Its checkout screens can’t be customized, so we designed around them.

BusinessThe bet was supply, so every choice had to make sellers more likely to turn shipping on.

Shipping adds to meetups. Sellers choose Sell nationwide, which keeps meetups open. Listings say Shipping available, not Shipping only.

No extra seller fees. Buyers pay shipping and a Buyer Protection Fee. Sellers pay nothing extra.

All of Canada. We launched everywhere at once, rather than Toronto or Ontario first.

CostShipping, fees and payouts all had a price.

A $10 floor. Below $10, shipping and fees cost more than the item.

Size before weight. Weight options start at 5 kg, because picking a size is easier than weighing. The cost is at most $1.59 undercharged on an order.

Legal and riskMoney between strangers brings fraud, chargebacks and consumer law.

Fees up front. Canada’s Competition Act bars mandatory fees that first appear at checkout, so the Buyer Protection Fee is named on the listing.

A $1,000 cap. Above it, fraud and chargeback losses grow. It also matches Vinted’s Buyer Protection limit.

Interac payouts. Bank transfers can be reversed for up to 90 days, so sellers are paid by Interac e-Transfer, with Karrot covering the fee.

What can ship. We blocked only what Canada Post prohibits, plus helmets, instead of whole categories, so electronics, fragile items and baby gear could ship.

Left for laterEach waited for a cost, a risk, or because it didn’t help answer the question.

What waited. Buy now in search and the home feed, buying inside a chat, carrier pickup at $3 or more per order, several items in one order, a brand for buyer protection, and the US, marketing and subsidies.

Two came back after launch. A Buy now button in chat once a seller accepts an offer, and bundles, which I started designing in October.

Solution

The first version

What shipped on September 16, for both sides of an order.

Buyers: one tap to buy, 48 hours to check

Buy now sits on the listing with every fee explained before checkout. Checkout shows an itemized total on one page, the order page tracks the item to the door, and the buyer has 48 hours after delivery to confirm it or report a problem.

Fees, explained. Tapping the fee on a listing explains shipping and the Buyer Protection Fee.

Checkout. Address, card and an itemized total on one page.

Order details. Paid, Shipped and Delivered, with Canada Post tracking.

A quick check. Confirm to pay the seller now, or report a problem. Otherwise payment releases on its own.

Sellers, part 1: shipping setup, with an LLM doing the hard part

Turning on shipping needs only a package size and a sender address. An LLM reads the listing and recommends the size, so the seller confirms it and adds an address once.

Two details. A package size and sender info are all shipping needs.

The LLM estimates. It reads the listing details and recommends a size.

Recommended. The suggested size is already filled in.

Easy to check. Each size compares to something familiar, like a shoebox, so the seller can confirm the pick or change it.

Sellers, part 2: after the sale, every next step spelled out

Once an item is paid, a system message in chat and the order page lead the seller from packing to payout. If the buyer reports a problem, it goes to the seller first, and the payout waits until it’s resolved.

Item sold. A message in chat with 5 days to ship and one button to start.

Order details. The ship-by date, shipping details and the label in one place.

Four steps to ship. Pack, take photos, print the label, drop off at Canada Post.

The seller responds. A partial refund, a full refund, or a dispute for Karrot to decide.

Deep dive

Package size, with less to think about

Choosing a package size was the step most likely to stop a seller. The buyer’s shipping cost depends on it. And unlike the US Postal Service, Canada Post gives out no free standard boxes, so Canadians reuse delivery boxes or pick one up at a grocery or liquor store.

I studied how retail apps explain fit and how marketplaces describe box sizes, then went through rounds of drafts and tests. Each round asked less of the seller.

A list of example items became one familiar object per size. Weight came off the size cards, since sellers pick a size or a weight, never both. And the size most items in the category need comes first, already selected.

Package size. Four sizes, each one thing people own. The likely size comes first, already selected.

No ruler needed. Printer paper for length and width, a thumb for height, and a shake test for padding.

Package weight. Each range shows real items, like a cast iron skillet or a PS5. Heavier ranges open on request.

For heavy categories, sellers pick a weight instead, up to 30 kg. I tested two ways to make kilograms feel real: one familiar anchor per range, like a 4 L jug of milk, or real items in each range. Real items shipped.

Behind each choice is what the label declares. Sizes use a dimensional weight, from 0.7 kg for S to 9.4 kg for XL, and weight ranges use their upper limit.

Results

Six weeks after launch

Orders grew, but three other numbers said more about where Buy now stood: how many listings could ship, what people bought, and how often a checkout ended in payment. Data as of late October 2026.

Supply

Sellers had to turn shipping on themselves, listing by listing.

80% of shipping setup visits ended with the setup saved, 14,374 of 18,048 from Oct 12 to 28. Once sellers opened setup, four in five finished.
146K eligible listings were posted from launch to mid-October. Sellers turned shipping on for 15.5K of them.
9.6% → 14% of eligible listings had shipping on, week by week. It stalled there, short of the 30% goal.
What sells

Buy now items cost more than a typical meetup sale, and most came from three categories.

$50 median Buy now item price, twice the $25 median of a single-item meetup sale.
71% of orders came from electronics (32%), women’s fashion (20%), and games and hobbies (19%).
47% is what shipping added to an item under $20, against 4% for items of $100 or more.
Checkout

People who reached checkout wanted the item, but most stopped at the total.

3.3% of checkout visits ended in payment, 153 of 4,642.
70% of visits stopped before any step: no address, no card, no Pay. Checkout was the first place buyers saw the full total with shipping, fees and tax.
83% of people who opened the address screen never tapped Pay.

The three pointed to the same place: the steps around a sale. Sellers needed help turning shipping on, buyers stopped at checkout, and the orders that did go through still had to be completed. The next round started there.

Iteration

Finding the friction

Shipping opt-in had stalled at 14%, but more listings wouldn’t help while a third of orders fell through. So I started with the hours after a sale, using two sources: every support conversation from Sep 24 to Nov 1, and every order placed from Sep 16 to Oct 28, read alongside its order chat.

Where people got stuck

636 help-chat messages and 101 support tickets in 39 days. The top question moved one step along the order every week or two: turning shipping on, then getting paid, then getting the box out to Canada Post.

Why orders fell through

75 of 238 orders were cancelled, and the rate was climbing: about 1 in 4 in September, 35 to 37% a week since mid-October. I looked at each cancelled order’s chat, the seller’s app visits and what happened to the listing afterwards.

47 of 75 ended on the seller side: 23 rejected, 24 never shipped.
35% vs 19% cancelled on a seller’s first Buy now order, against repeat sellers.
54% of orders of $150 or more were cancelled. They hold 70% of the value lost.

Sellers weren’t ready for a real order

22 of 23 rejections were the seller’s first Buy now order, and 20 came within a day of payment. The chats show items already sold elsewhere. Orders with no chat before payment were cancelled more often, 38% against 27%.

Sellers saw the order and still didn’t ship

23 of the 24 sellers who never shipped opened the app during the five days, and 19 wrote to the buyer. 12 never opened the label. The chats mention no printer, or wanting to meet up instead. Every one of those buyers waited the full five days for a refund.

What we’re changing

Sellers had the order and the app open, but the next step was buried in a chat, and the step after it assumed a printer. Earlier follow-ups covered questions and setup: a How shipping works guide with an AI help chat, shipping for every listing at once, and a way for buyers to ask for shipping. This round starts with the hours after a sale.

1

Put the sale where sellers already look

For the sellers who saw the order and didn’t ship. One order shows once per screen, sorted by ship-by date, up to three before See more, and leaves when it’s done.

Home. A sold item sits at the top of the feed. A first-time seller is offered the steps, a repeat seller goes straight to shipping.

Chats. The order chat stays pinned above the rest, with its deadline in place of the last message.

Chatroom. A Next step bar keeps the action one tap away while the two people talk.

My Karrot. A To do list for what the seller owes, next to purchases still on their way.

2

Every step spelled out, and no printer needed

For first-time sellers, the 12 who never opened the label, and the sellers with no printer.

Get ready to ship. Four steps before the first box: pack, photos if they like, the QR code, then drop-off.

A QR code before a printer. Sellers show a code at the counter. Printing becomes the fallback.

A timeline, not a chat. I chose one vertical timeline over chat-style bubbles. Sellers see whose turn it is, the next action, and when they’ll be paid.

Last day. On the final day the card turns into a warning, with a time instead of a date.

Still open: decisions, not screens

Some fixes aren’t design. Seller protection needs a decision before any screen can promise it. Debit cards need one honest sentence at checkout. Twelve buyers who only want local, two of them ready to leave, need an answer on hiding shipping listings. Each is written up with its evidence, waiting on a call.

What’s next

Ship the seller to-dos and the step-by-step guide, then watch whether seller-side cancellations fall, starting with first orders. Then whether shipping listings can sell as well as local ones. The question hasn’t changed: can Buy now grow Karrot in Canada? Now we have a product to answer it with.
